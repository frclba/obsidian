Microeconomics zooms in on how individuals and firms make decisions. If macroeconomics is the weather, microeconomics is the thermometer in your backyard.

The core building blocks:

**Supply and Demand** — the foundational model. When something is scarce and desired, its price goes up. When it's abundant, the price drops. The intersection of supply and demand curves sets the market price. Elegant and surprisingly powerful for such a simple model.

**Marginal thinking** — decisions are made at the margin, not in totals. You don't decide whether water is valuable in the abstract — you decide whether *one more glass* of water is worth the price. This is why diamonds cost more than water despite water being more "essential."

**Elasticity** — how sensitive is demand to price changes? Insulin is inelastic (people need it regardless of price). Luxury goods are elastic (raise the price and people switch to alternatives). Understanding elasticity tells you who has power in a market.

**Market structures:**
- **Perfect competition** — many sellers, identical products, no one has pricing power. Closest real example: commodity agriculture.
- **Monopoly** — one seller, total pricing power. Rare in pure form but common in modified forms (tech platforms, local utilities).
- **Oligopoly** — a few large players who watch each other closely. Airlines, telecom, banking. This is where [[game theory]] really matters.
- **Monopolistic competition** — many sellers with slightly differentiated products. Restaurants, clothing brands.

**Externalities and market failures** — markets don't always produce optimal outcomes. Pollution, information asymmetry, public goods, and natural monopolies are all cases where markets alone can't solve the problem.

Microeconomics gives you a mental framework for understanding any market, any negotiation, any business decision. It's not about math — it's about understanding incentives and trade-offs.
