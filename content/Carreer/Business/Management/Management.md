# Management

Management is one of those words that gets thrown around so much it's almost lost its meaning. But at its core, management is about getting things done through people. Not doing everything yourself, not micromanaging every detail, but creating the conditions where a group of people can do their best work together toward a shared goal.

The discipline branches out in a lot of directions. [[Product Management]] is about figuring out *what* to build and *why* -- understanding customer needs, market dynamics, and making the hard calls about what gets prioritized. [[Project Managment]] is more about the *how* and *when* -- making sure the thing actually gets delivered on time and within constraints. They overlap a lot, but the mindsets are different. Product managers think like strategists, project managers think like orchestrators.

One of the hardest parts of management that nobody prepares you for is [[Hiring and firing]]. Hiring well is probably the single highest-leverage activity a manager can do. One great hire can transform a team. One bad hire can poison it. And firing -- when it's necessary -- is one of the most emotionally difficult things you'll ever do in a professional context. But keeping someone who isn't working out is unfair to them, unfair to the team, and unfair to you. The best managers learn to make these calls quickly and compassionately.

[[Business management]] zooms out even further and looks at the whole organism -- strategy, finance, operations, culture. It's the 30,000-foot view where you're thinking about competitive positioning, resource allocation, and long-term sustainability. This is where management becomes less about day-to-day execution and more about vision and direction.

Here's what I think most people get wrong about management: they think it's about control. It's not. The best managers I've seen are the ones who set clear expectations, remove obstacles, and then get out of the way. They hire people smarter than themselves and give them the autonomy to do great work. They provide context instead of commands. They ask "what do you need from me?" more than they say "here's what I need from you."

The other thing that separates great managers from mediocre ones is their relationship with feedback. They give it constantly -- not just in quarterly reviews but in real-time, specific, and actionable doses. And more importantly, they actively seek it themselves. A manager who can't hear honest feedback from their team is flying blind, no matter how talented they are.

Management is a craft. Like any craft, you get better at it with deliberate practice, reflection, and a genuine desire to serve the people you lead. The moment you start thinking of management as a title rather than a responsibility, you've already started failing at it.
